## Executive Summary

**Current State**: Your organization is at Maturity Level 2 - You've established foundational AI governance with documented policies, assigned responsibilities, and emerging processes. However, adoption is inconsistent and enforcement is manual.

**Target State**: Maturity Level 3 - Achieve enterprise-wide standardization where AI governance is embedded in organizational processes, consistently applied across all projects, and supported by automation and tooling.

**Timeline**: **12-18 months** to solidly achieve Maturity Level 3 (varies by organizational size and complexity)

**Investment Level**: **Significant**
- 1-2 FTE dedicated to AI governance and MLOps platform
- $150-500K in platforms, tools, and integration (MLOps, governance tooling, training)
- 15-25% time allocation from cross-functional teams (technical, governance, change management)

**Key Priorities**:
1. **Standardization**: Transform documented processes into organization-wide standards
2. **Automation**: Implement MLOps platform and governance automation
3. **Integration**: Embed AI governance into existing enterprise frameworks
4. **Enforcement**: Move from voluntary adoption to systematic compliance
5. **Scaling**: Enable organization-wide AI deployment with confidence

**Success Indicator**: Within 18 months, AI governance is "the way we do things" - new AI projects automatically follow standards, processes are automated, and exceptions are rare.

## 1. Current State Context: Understanding Maturity Level 2

### Where You Are and Why That's an Achievement

**You're at Maturity Level 2 if**:
- AI governance structure exists (committee, policies, assigned roles)
- Basic AI inventory is maintained
- AI development processes are documented
- Risk assessment is conducted for some projects
- Model documentation template exists
- Some production models have monitoring
- Leadership receives periodic AI updates

**This is real progress**. You've accomplished what most organizations haven't - establishing visibility and structure. **You should be proud of getting here.**

### The Maturity Level 2 Reality: Foundation But Not Consistency

**What's working**:
- You have the building blocks (policies, processes, templates)
- Leadership is aware and supportive
- Technical teams know what "good" looks like
- You can point to examples of proper AI governance

**What's frustrating**:
- **Inconsistent adoption**: Some teams follow processes, others don't
- **Manual overhead**: Everything requires individual follow-up
- **Enforcement gaps**: No systematic way to ensure compliance
- **Tool fragmentation**: Teams use different platforms and approaches
- **Scalability limits**: Current approach won't work for 10x the AI projects

**This is normal for Maturity Level 2**. You've built the foundation. Now it's time to systematize and scale.

### Common Maturity Level 2 Frustrations

**Frustration A: "We have policies, but teams still do their own thing"**
Reality: Documentation without enforcement is just documentation. Maturity Level 3 is about making standards non-optional through integration and automation.

**Frustration B: "Governance feels like extra work"**
Reality: When governance is manual and separate from workflow, it's friction. Maturity Level 3 embeds governance into tools so it becomes invisible.

**Frustration C: "We can't scale with manual processes"**
Reality: Maturity Level 2 processes work for 5-10 AI projects. At 50+ projects, they break. Maturity Level 3 automation enables scaling.

**Frustration D: "Different teams have different tool stacks"**
Reality: Tool fragmentation is expensive and creates governance gaps. Maturity Level 3 standardizes on enterprise platforms.

### Why Organizations Get Stuck at Maturity Level 2

**Common obstacles**:

1. **Budget Constraints**: Moving to Maturity Level 3 requires platform investment ($150-500K) that may compete with feature development
2. **Organizational Politics**: Standardization means some teams have to give up their preferred tools or approaches
3. **Change Fatigue**: Your organization just implemented Maturity Level 2 governance; stakeholders may resist "more governance work"
4. **Technical Complexity**: Integrating AI governance into existing IT, security, and compliance systems is complex
5. **Skills Gaps**: Enterprise MLOps and governance automation require specialized skills you may not have
6. **Fear of Slowing Down**: Teams worry that more structure will kill agility and innovation

**None of these are insurmountable**. But they require **intentional change management and sustained commitment**.

## 2. The Case for Change: Why Move to Maturity Level 3?

### Risks of Remaining at Maturity Level 2

Maturity Level 2 is a good intermediate state, but **staying here long-term creates problems**:

**Scalability Risks**:
- **Can't grow AI usage**: Manual processes collapse under volume
- **Innovation bottleneck**: Governance becomes a team bottleneck rather than enabler
- **Competitive disadvantage**: Competitors who automate governance move faster

**Compliance Risks**:
- **Audit failures**: Inconsistent processes mean you can't demonstrate compliance systematically
- **Regulatory gaps**: As AI regulations tighten (EU AI Act, industry standards), ad-hoc governance won't suffice
- **Certification barriers**: ISO 42001 or SOC 2 AI certification requires Maturity Level 3 systematic processes

**Operational Risks**:
- **Technical debt accumulation**: Tool fragmentation creates maintenance nightmares
- **Talent attrition**: Good practitioners get frustrated with inconsistent, manual processes
- **Incident exposure**: Governance gaps mean risks slip through cracks

**Business Risks**:
- **Lost opportunities**: Can't move fast on AI-driven business opportunities because governance can't keep up
- **Customer trust issues**: Customers and partners expect enterprise-grade AI governance
- **Board/investor concerns**: Can't demonstrate systematic risk management

### Challenges You'll Face Moving to Maturity Level 3

**Let's be honest about the hard parts**:

1. **Platform Investment and ROI Questions**
   - Challenge: $150-500K for ML Ops platform is a significant ask
   - Reality: This is infrastructure investment, like your ERP or CRM. ROI comes from scale and risk reduction.
   - Mitigation: Build TCO analysis showing cost of NOT investing (manual labor, incidents, lost opportunities)

2. **Tool Standardization Battles**
   - Challenge: Data scientists love their tools. Standardization feels like loss of autonomy.
   - Reality: Standardization doesn't mean one tool for everything. It means **interoperable enterprise platforms**.
   - Mitigation: Involve practitioners in platform selection. Show how platforms enhance (not restrict) their capabilities.

3. **Change Management at Scale**
   - Challenge: Maturity Level 2→3 affects the entire organization, not just AI teams
   - Reality: This is enterprise-wide transformation requiring sustained communication and training
   - Mitigation: Dedicate resources to change management. Celebrate early adopters. Address concerns proactively.

4. **Integration Complexity**
   - Challenge: AI governance must integrate with IT, security, compliance, procurement, HR systems
   - Reality: This takes time and coordination across enterprise architecture teams
   - Mitigation: Phase integration. Start with highest-value integrations (e.g., HR for talent management, IT for deployment)

5. **Organizational Maturity Ceiling**
   - Challenge: If your org struggles with enterprise standards generally, AI won't be different
   - Reality: Maturity Level 3 AI maturity requires Maturity Level 3 organizational maturity (documented, standardized processes)
   - Mitigation: Acknowledge this dependency. AI governance can be a **catalyst** for broader organizational maturity.

6. **Fear of Bureaucracy**
   - Challenge: Teams worry Maturity Level 3 means "enterprise bureaucracy" killing innovation
   - Reality: Well-implemented Maturity Level 3 is **invisible governance** - automated, integrated, and enabling
   - Mitigation: Show examples of what good looks like (internal demos, industry case studies)

### Time Realities

**What happens in 6 months**:
- Platform selected and procured
- Integration architecture designed
- Pilot teams onboarded to new platforms
- First automated governance workflows operational

**What takes 12-18 months**:
- Enterprise-wide platform adoption
- Full integration with existing systems
- Cultural shift from "extra work" to "how we work"
- Measurement showing governance enables speed, not slows it

**Maturity Level 2→3 is longer than Maturity Level 1→2** because you're changing not just documentation, but **organizational behavior and system architecture**. That's structural change, which takes time.

## 3. Target State Vision: What Maturity Level 3 and Maturity Level 4 Look Like

### Maturity Level 3 (Your Goal): Enterprise Standards

**You'll know you've reached Maturity Level 3 when**:

✅ **Standardized Processes**
- All AI projects follow documented, organization-wide process (no exceptions without explicit approval)
- AI governance integrated into existing project management, IT, and compliance workflows
- Model documentation, risk assessment, and approval are **automatic parts** of AI lifecycle, not bolt-ons

✅ **Platform and Tools**
- Enterprise MLOps platform in place (model development, deployment, monitoring, governance)
- Centralized model registry and AI system catalog
- Automated governance checks (policy compliance, bias detection, security scans) as part of CI/CD
- Unified observability and monitoring for all AI systems

✅ **Embedded Governance**
- AI governance policies integrated into enterprise risk management and compliance frameworks
- Regular audits confirm compliance (and findings show high compliance rates)
- AI ethics review integrated into high-risk AI project approvals
- Third-party AI vendor management integrated into procurement

✅ **Organization-Wide Capability**
- AI literacy is universal (training is part of onboarding for all relevant roles)
- Cross-functional collaboration is standard (projects automatically include governance, legal, security)
- AI centers of excellence or communities of practice are active and well-attended
- Knowledge management captures and shares AI lessons learned systematically

✅ **Measurement**
- AI governance metrics tracked and reported regularly (compliance rates, incident rates, time-to-deployment)
- Process effectiveness is measured and drives continuous improvement
- ROI of AI initiatives is tracked systematically

**Maturity Level 3 is about being systematic**. Governance isn't an extra step - it's embedded. Standards aren't suggestions - they're enforced. AI is enterprise capability, not skunkworks.

### Maturity Level 4 (

The Future): Data-Driven Management

**Looking ahead to Maturity Level 4** (18-36 months from now):

At Maturity Level 4, what's standardized at Maturity Level 3 becomes **quantitatively measured and predictively managed**:
- **Predictive risk management**: ML models predict which AI systems are likely to have issues before they occur
- **Automated optimization**: Systems automatically tune governance controls based on measured effectiveness
- **Real-time dashboards**: Leadership sees AI performance, risk, and value metrics in real-time
- **Continuous validation**: AI systems continuously validate trustworthy characteristics (bias, fairness, explainability)
- **Benchmarking**: Your AI maturity is measured against industry standards and you know where you stand

**Why show you Maturity Level 4 now?** When you're asking for platform investment and organizational change, stakeholders need to see the **long-term strategic value**. Maturity Level 3 enables Maturity Level 4's data-driven excellence.

## 4. Communication Strategy for Implementation Leaders

### Your Challenge: Balancing Standardization with Innovation

As the change agent, you need to:
- **Be clear** about Maturity Level 3 requirements (enterprise standards, platform adoption, integrated governance)
- **Articulate benefits** of Maturity Level 3 (scalability, risk reduction, competitive advantage)
- **Paint vision** of Maturity Level 4 (data-driven AI management, industry leadership)
- **Address fears** that standardization kills innovation (show it enables innovation at scale)

### How to Talk About Maturity Level 3 Requirements

**With Executives**:
- ❌ Don't say: "We need to implement strict AI controls and more governance processes"
- ✅ Do say: "We need to invest in enterprise AI platforms that automate governance and enable scaling from 10 to 100+ AI projects safely and efficiently."

**With Technical Teams**:
- ❌ Don't say: "You can no longer use your preferred tools"
- ✅ Do say: "We're implementing an enterprise MLOps platform that integrates with your workflows, automates the boring parts, and lets you focus on model development."

**With Business Leaders**:
- ❌ Don't say: "AI projects will take longer due to new governance requirements"
- ✅ Do say: "We're automating governance so AI projects can scale faster while maintaining trust and compliance."

### Handling Resistance:

The Innovation vs. Control Tension

**Resistance Type**: "Standardization will slow down innovation"

**Response**: "I understand that concern - no one wants to kill innovation. But think about what slows us down now: manual governance reviews, inconsistent tools creating integration problems, reinventing solutions each team already solved, and incidents that could have been prevented. Maturity Level 3 automates the boring parts and provides reusable components so teams can innovate **faster**. Companies like Netflix, Uber, and Airbnb didn't become AI leaders by having everyone do their own thing - they built platforms that enabled innovation at scale."

**Resistance Type**: "We just finished implementing Maturity Level 2 - why more change?"

**Response**: "Maturity Level 2 was about establishing visibility and documentation - and we've done that successfully. But our teams are already hitting limits: manual processes don't scale, inconsistent adoption creates risks, and we're getting feedback that governance feels like extra work. Maturity Level 3 isn't 'more governance' - it's **automating** what we built in Maturity Level 2 so it becomes invisible and scales. Think of it as Maturity Level 2 graduation, not Maturity Level 2 rejection."

**Resistance Type**: "Platform investment is expensive and the ROI is unclear"

**Response**: "Let's look at the cost of NOT investing: [Calculate: hours spent on manual governance × hourly cost + risk of incidents + opportunity cost of projects we can't pursue because governance doesn't scale]. That's our current state tax. The platform investment pays for itself in [12-24] months through efficiency gains alone - and that's before we factor in risk reduction and the ability to scale AI to new use cases that drive revenue."

## 5. Multi-Audience Assessments

### How to Use These Assessments

Each audience completes its own assessment independently before the transition begins, and again at Month 9 and Month 18. The questions are written for this transition specifically: they ask whether a capability is **organization-wide and consistent** (ML3), not whether it exists at all. An organization still scoring 0 or 1 on most of these is not ready for the enterprise rollout this guide describes, however much of the Maturity Level 1→2 work it has finished.

Every question carries an **Evidence to Look For** line. Score from the evidence, not from the intention: if the evidence cannot be produced in a few minutes, the score is not 2.

### Assessment Instructions

Use these assessments to confirm you're ready for Maturity Level 2→3 progression and to track progress over 18 months.

**Scoring**: canonical 0-4 / ML1-ML5 scale (see `../AIMF-ASSESSMENT-GUIDE.md`):
- 0 = ML1 - Initial
- 1 = ML2 - Developing (this guide's starting point)
- 2 = ML3 - Defined (this guide's target)
- 3 = ML4 - Managed
- 4 = ML5 - Optimizing

**Maturity Level 3 Target**: Most questions should score **2** (ML3 - Defined, organization-wide and consistent).

If most questions currently score 1 (ML2 - Developing), you're at solid Maturity Level 2 and ready for this guide.

> **Scale note**: the retired 0-3 lens scored this quiz 0-3 with "3 = Established". "Established, organization-wide" is ML3 = **2** on the canonical scale, so the ML2 baseline above is **1** and the ML3 target is **2**. Old quiz notes convert with the table in `../AIMF-ASSESSMENT-GUIDE.md`.

### Technical Playbook: MLOps Platform and Automation

**Your Role**: Technical teams implement the MLOps platform, automate governance, and drive adoption.

#### Priority Actions (Months 1-6)

**Action 1: Select and Procure the Enterprise MLOps Platform**

**What**: Evaluate candidate platforms against a weighted, published shortlist of criteria, then choose on evidence rather than vendor demo quality.

**How**:
1. Publish the criteria and their weights before any vendor meeting (use the criteria table in §7)
2. Issue the same written requirements document to every shortlisted vendor and require written answers
3. Run a proof-of-concept on three representative workloads: one batch model, one LLM/RAG application, one high-risk model under the strictest control tier
4. Score each candidate against the criteria with named scorers, recording disagreements rather than averaging them away
5. Test the things vendors do not demo: export of inventory data, audit trail completeness, IAM integration, exit cost
6. Record the decision and its rationale, including the criteria that could not be satisfied

**Deliverable**: Evaluation report with scoring, dissenting views and a documented decision

**Timeline**: Month 1-2
**Investment**: 120-200 hours across platform, security and governance teams
**Action 2: Design and Implement the Model Registry**

**What**: Implement the registry that becomes the single source of truth for what AI systems exist, at what version, approved by whom.

**How**:
1. Define the minimum metadata set per model: control ID reference, business owner, technical owner, purpose, training data lineage reference, evaluation results, risk tier, approval record
2. Integrate registration into the deployment pipeline so an unregistered artefact cannot deploy (see Action 3)
3. Backfill the existing portfolio: every production model gets registered, with gaps marked as gaps
4. Give governance read access and the reporting queries it needs, replacing manual inventory collection
5. Define retirement handling: superseded versions remain recorded with date and reason
6. Test the registry against the current inventory and reconcile every difference

**Deliverable**: Registry in production, portfolio backfilled, manual inventory retired

**Timeline**: Month 2-4
**Investment**: 160-240 hours (higher if the portfolio has never been inventoried)

---

**Action 3: Automate Model Deployment with Governance Gates**

**What**: Move deployment onto pipelines that enforce the checks the governance framework requires, so compliance stops depending on memory.

**How**:
1. Codify the release checklist as pipeline stages: tests, documentation completeness, risk assessment reference, approval record, evaluation within tolerance
2. Make the pipeline the only route to production; issue manual deployment exceptions through the exception process with an expiry date
3. Record, per deployment, the approving identity, the change set and the checks passed — this record becomes control evidence
4. Implement automated rollback and test it in a game day, not during an incident
5. Instrument gate failures: a gate that always passes is either unnecessary or not working, and both are findings

**Deliverable**: Pipeline-enforced release process with evidence records and tested rollback

**Timeline**: Month 3-6
**Investment**: 200-320 hours plus platform engineering time

---

**Action 4: Implement Comprehensive Model Monitoring**

**What**: Put every production system under defined monitoring with thresholds agreed with its business owner.

**How**:
1. For each system, define the metrics that matter: task performance, input distribution drift, output behaviour, latency, error rate, cost per transaction
2. Agree thresholds with the business owner — the owner, not the engineering team, decides what counts as unacceptable performance
3. Route alerts to a named on-call owner with response-time expectations by severity
4. Build one portfolio-level view so governance can see coverage and blind spots, not just per-system dashboards
5. Review alert precision quarterly: too many false alerts trains people to ignore the real one

**Deliverable**: Monitoring live for all production systems, coverage reported to governance

**Timeline**: Month 4-6 for the highest-risk systems, Month 7-12 for the remainder
**Investment**: 40-80 hours per system plus platform cost

### Governance Playbook: Standardization and Assurance

**Your Role**: Governance moves from *writing the framework* (Maturity Level 2) to *making it the way work is done* — one policy set, standardized risk assessment, and assurance that tests rather than assumes.

#### Priority Actions (Months 1-6)

**Action 1: Consolidate the Policy Set**

**What**: Reduce the AI policy estate to one authoritative framework with documented deviations.

**How**:
1. Inventory every AI-related policy, standard and guideline in circulation, including team-level documents
2. Identify conflicts and duplicates; record each one rather than silently merging
3. Publish the consolidated framework with scope, ownership, review cadence and version control
4. Convert local variants into registered deviations with an owner, a reason and an expiry date
5. Communicate the effective date and retire superseded documents explicitly

**Timeline**: Month 1-3
**Investment**: 60-100 hours policy work plus stakeholder review

---

**Action 2: Standardize and Tier the Risk Assessment**

**What**: Apply one risk method with tier-based depth so that a high-risk system cannot be assessed as lightly as a low-risk one.

**How**:
1. Define the method: impact dimensions, likelihood scale, decision rules for tier assignment
2. Map each tier to the assessment depth and control set it requires
3. Re-assess the existing portfolio against the method and record tier changes
4. Institute a consistency check: have a second reviewer re-assess a sample and compare
5. Publish the method to delivery teams so the assessment is predictable

**Timeline**: Month 2-4
**Investment**: 80-140 hours plus re-assessment effort

---

**Action 3: Build the Control-to-Obligation Map**

**What**: Make it possible to answer "which control satisfies NIST GOVERN 1.2, ISO/IEC 42001 clause 6.1.4, or this contract clause?"

**How**:
1. Start from the AIMF control catalogue — it is already mapped to NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001 and ISO/IEC 27001 (see `../../mappings/`)
2. Add your own obligations: contracts, sector regulation, internal commitments
3. Record the evidence each control requires and who owns it
4. Identify obligations with no satisfying control and treat them as gaps with owners
5. Set the review cadence at which the map is reconciled with the obligation register

**Timeline**: Month 2-5
**Investment**: 100-160 hours plus legal/compliance review

---

**Action 4: Launch the Assurance Programme**

**What**: Test controls independently and report findings where they will be acted on.

**How**:
1. Build the assurance plan risk-first: highest-risk systems and most load-bearing controls in year one
2. Define testing method and sampling for each control type (evidence inspection, walkthrough, re-performance)
3. Assign testing to a function independent of the control owner
4. Rate findings and track to closure with evidence of remediation
5. Report findings and repeat findings to the governance committee and the board cycle

**Timeline**: Month 3-6
**Investment**: 100-200 hours per cycle, or external cost if outsourced

---

#### Ongoing Activities (Months 6-18)

**Quarterly**:
- Reconcile the obligation register with the control map and close gaps
- Review assurance findings, repeat findings and remediation status
- Review third-party AI provider changes and reassessments due
- Report governance effectiveness to the board/audit committee cycle

**Annually**:
- Full review of the policy set and deviation register
- Refresh of the risk method against regulatory change
- Assurance plan for the coming year

### Change Agent Playbook: Adoption at Enterprise Scale

**Your Role**: At Maturity Level 2→3 the constraints stop being *technical* and become *adoption*: teams keep their old route because it is faster until the new route is easier. Your job is to make the standard the path of least resistance.

#### Priority Actions (Months 1-6)

**Action 1: Measure Adoption Before Asking for It**

**What**: Establish per-team adoption baselines so the programme can target support where it is needed.

**How**:
1. Define three to five adoption measures that are collected automatically (pipeline usage, registry coverage, documentation completeness, review completion)
2. Publish baseline by team and by system — visible to the teams themselves first
3. Agree targets with each team lead rather than announcing them
4. Review monthly with the teams, using the same data they see

**Timeline**: Month 1-2
**Investment**: 40-60 hours plus dashboard work

**Action 2: Build the Champion Network**

**What**: Establish named champions per team with a real remit, then use them as the primary feedback channel.

**How**:
1. Recruit one champion per team or business unit, ideally the person others already ask
2. Give them a written remit and protected time (10-20% is realistic)
3. Brief them before general rollout on every change
4. Run a monthly forum where friction is reported upward and answered
5. Recognise contribution publicly; a champion network without recognition decays within two quarters

**Timeline**: Month 2-4
**Investment**: 20-30 hours setup plus recurring forum time

---

**Action 3: Remove Friction the Data Points To**

**What**: Treat exception and bypass trends as defect reports against the process, not as discipline problems.

**How**:
1. Log every exception request with its stated reason
2. Analyse reasons monthly: unclear criteria, slow approvals, missing tooling, or genuine risk
3. Fix the process cause where it exists, and record the fix
4. Where the standard genuinely does not fit a case, add a documented, bounded deviation path
5. Report the trend to governance — a falling exception rate is the evidence that adoption is real

**Timeline**: Month 3-6 and ongoing
**Investment**: 20-40 hours per cycle

**Action 4: Deliver Role-Based Enablement**

**What**: Replace general awareness training with role-specific enablement at the point of need.

**How**:
1. Define curricula per role: engineer, reviewer, business owner, executive
2. Embed guidance in the workflow (templates, gate checklists, examples of a passing record)
3. Track completion and, more importantly, effectiveness (error rates at the gate, rework rates)
4. Add refreshers on a cadence and an onboarding path for new joiners

**Timeline**: Month 2-6
**Investment**: 80-120 hours content development plus delivery

**Monthly**:
- Review adoption by team with the champion network
- Review friction and exception trends and assign fixes

**Quarterly**:
- Report adoption and programme progress to leadership with the same rigour as delivery metrics
- Update enablement content for changes to standards

**Annually**:
- Re-baseline adoption measures

## 7. Cross-Functional Elements

### Platform Selection Criteria

**What to score, and how to weight it**: the criteria below are the ones that decide whether a platform reduces governance cost or merely relocates it. Weight them before you meet any vendor; a demonstration that scores well on the unweighted list usually scores badly once audit-trail and exit cost are weighted.

| # | Criterion | Weight | The question that actually discriminates |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Governance metadata coverage** | 20% | Can the registry hold every field the framework's control evidence requires (purpose, owner, data lineage, evaluation, approval, risk tier) without a side spreadsheet? |
| 2 | **Audit trail completeness** | 15% | Does every model change, approval and deployment leave an immutable record that can be exported for an auditor? |
| 3 | **Pipeline enforcement** | 15% | Can the platform **block** a deployment that fails a governance check, or does it only report? |
| 4 | **Integration with enterprise identity and GRC** | 10% | Does it use your SSO/SCIM and can it exchange data with your GRC tool, or does it become a second identity and risk silo? |
| 5 | **Third-party and foundation-model handling** | 10% | Can it represent an externally hosted model as a first-class governed asset, including provider version changes? |
| 6 | **Exit cost and data portability** | 10% | Can you export the full registry, audit trail and lineage in a documented format, and what does migration actually cost? |
| 7 | **Operational cost model** | 10% | What does the bill look like at 3× current volume, and which meter drives it (compute, seats, models, registrations)? |
| 8 | **Extensibility** | 5% | Can your governance team add a control, a gate or a report without raising a vendor change request? |
| 9 | **Security posture** | 5% | Does it meet the same security standard you would apply to any system holding model and data lineage? |

**Rules that keep the evaluation honest**:
- Publish the weights and the requirements document before the first vendor conversation
- Require written answers to the requirements document; score the writing, not the demo
- Score criterion 6 (exit) with procurement in the room, not the platform team alone
- Record dissenting scores; an averaged score hides exactly the disagreement that matters at contract time
- A platform that scores badly on 1, 2, 3 or 6 cannot be compensated for by strength elsewhere — those four are what "Maturity Level 3" means operationally

### Integration Architecture

The platform does not replace the surrounding enterprise systems; it has to interoperate with them, or governance data fragments the moment a second tool is added. The reference integration for a Maturity Level 3 estate has five links:

1. **Identity** — AI platform accounts derive from the corporate directory (SSO + SCIM). No local accounts, because local accounts are unowned accounts.
2. **Source control and CI** — every model artefact and pipeline definition is tied to a commit. The registry records the commit, not just the artefact name; this is what makes a deployment reproducible.
3. **GRC / risk register** — the AI risk assessment record created for each system is linked from the registry by identifier, so the control evidence and the risk assessment are never reconciled by hand.
4. **ITSM** — AI incidents enter the standard incident process with an AI-specific category, so trend reporting covers AI incidents alongside everything else instead of living in a separate spreadsheet.
5. **BI / data warehouse** — monitoring and registry metadata flow to the warehouse for the portfolio dashboards described in the Maturity Level 3→4 guide; the executive view should never depend on someone exporting a CSV.

**Data direction matters**: registry and control metadata are the system of record and flow *out*; risk assessments, incidents and change records are systems of record elsewhere and flow *in* by reference. Duplicating either direction creates two versions of the truth, which is the Maturity Level 2 problem this transition exists to fix.

### Common Failure Modes

| Failure | What it looks like | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Platform bought, adoption not** | The platform is live; half the portfolio still deploys by hand | Gate the pipeline (Technical Action 3) and measure adoption per team (Change Agent Action 1) — the mandate alone does not move this |
| **Registry that is a spreadsheet with an API** | Every field is manually maintained; registration is a form people forget | Make registration a pipeline side effect, not a separate task |
| **Governance theatre** | Gates configured to pass; review approvals given without reading | Instrument gate failures and review refusal rates; a gate that never refuses is not a control |
| **Standards written for the largest team** | Small teams route around them; exception volume climbs | Document bounded deviations and risk-tier the control depth |
| **Assurance findings with no owner** | The same finding reported in three consecutive cycles | Rate findings, assign owners, and escalate repeats to the executive owner |

### Timeline and Effort Estimates

**Overall Timeline**: 18 months from Maturity Level 2 to solidly achieving Maturity Level 3

**Phasing**:
- **Months 1-6** (Platform): Select, procure, and implement platform with pilot teams
- **Months 7-12** (Adoption): Enterprise-wide rollout and integration
- **Months 13-18** (Maturity): Full adoption, enforcement, and optimization

### Pitfalls Specific to Maturity Level 2→3

1. **Buying the platform as the deliverable.** The platform is infrastructure; the deliverable is that governance checks are enforced and evidenced. If the platform is live and adoption is 40%, the transition has not happened.
2. **Treating the registry as a document.** A registry that is updated by hand is an inventory with extra steps and will be stale within a quarter.
3. **Writing standards that only the mature teams can meet.** Risk-tier the control depth, and give small teams a documented deviation path instead of an exception queue.
4. **Measuring maturity by artefacts produced.** Twelve policies are not maturity; one policy that is attested and tested is.
5. **Declaring Maturity Level 3 at the first dashboard.** Maturity Level 3 requires evidence that the controls operate, and the assessment guide's evidence tests are the arbiter — not the existence of the tooling.
